Here are 5 options for a 1-2 minute opening speech for your meeting on architectural characteristics, designed to be catchy and use the storytelling techniques from our summary.

---

### Option 1: The Three Restaurants (User's Analogy)

**(Techniques: Analogy, Zooming In, Raising Questions)**

"Good morning. I want you to picture three places I visited last week.

On Tuesday, I walked into a fine-dining restaurant. **(Location)** White tablecloths, a wine list as thick as a book, and a waiter who described every dish like a work of art. The food was incredible, but it took an hour.

On Friday, I was in a hurry, so I hit a fast-food drive-thru. **(Action)** I shouted my order into a speaker, had my food in three minutes, and it tasted exactly like I expected. Lightning fast.

On Sunday, I went to my university's cafeteria. Hundreds of people, dozens of options from salads to pizza, all served buffet-style. It fed an army of students in under an hour.

Three successful businesses. But... could the fine-dining restaurant serve a hundred people in an hour? Could the fast-food joint create a unique, five-course tasting menu? Could the cafeteria deliver a single, perfect, hot meal to your car window?

No. Of course not.

They were all *architected* differently, for different goals. One for **experience**, one for **speed**, one for **scale**.

Today, we're here to ask: what kind of restaurant are we building? We have a menu of features, but we haven't defined the architectural characteristics that will make us great. Let's figure that out."

---

### Option 2: The Two Phone Calls (Mystery & Dialogue)

**(Techniques: Raising Questions, Dialogue, Inner Thoughts)**

"I had two phone calls last week that I can't shake.

The first was with Sarah, a brand new customer. She said, **(Dialogue)** 'I am *in love* with this platform! It's so powerful, I feel like I can build anything with it. The possibilities are endless!' I got off that call feeling great.

The next day, I talked to David, a power user who's been with us for five years. He told me, **(Dialogue)** 'I'm done. I'm leaving. Your platform has gotten so slow and bloated that my team wastes more time waiting for it to load than actually working. We can't rely on it.'

My first thought was, **(Inner Thought)** 'How can they both be right?' How can one person's 'powerful' be another's 'bloated'? How can 'endless possibilities' for one user mean 'unreliable' for another?

That contradiction is why we are in this room for the next six hours. We have focused entirely on *what* our product can do, but we've ignored *how* it does it. We've built features without defining its core characteristics. Today, we define them."

---

### Option 3: The Skyscraper Foundation (Analogy & Stakes)

**(Techniques: Analogy, Establishing Stakes, Showing Emotion)**

"Imagine we're a team of architects designing a skyscraper.

For the last year, we've designed the most stunning lobby, the fastest glass elevators, and corner offices with breathtaking views. We've obsessed over the user-facing features.

But we never stopped to ask the most important questions. Is this skyscraper being built in a quiet field, or is it in downtown Tokyo, an active earthquake zone? Is it designed for a hundred people, or ten thousand?

Because if it's in an earthquake zone, the most critical characteristic isn't the view—it's **resilience**. If it's for ten thousand people, it's **scalability**. The answers to those questions define the foundation. And if we get the foundation wrong, I feel a pit in my stomach **(Showing Emotion)** because our beautiful lobby is just rubble waiting to happen.

Our features are the lobby and the offices. Today, for the first time, we're here to architect the foundation. Let's make sure it's one that will last."

---

### Option 4: The 2 AM Alert (Personal Story & Emotion)

**(Techniques: Zooming in, Inner Dialogue, Showing Emotion)**

"Two weeks ago, my phone lit up my bedroom at 2:17 AM. **(Location & Action)**

It was a PagerDuty alert. The system was down.

As I reached for my laptop, my shoulders tensed up **(Showing Emotion)**, and I had one, crystal-clear thought: **(Inner Thought)** 'It's the analytics module again. I bet the nightly reports are running and it's falling over. Again.'

And I was right.

The most telling part of that story isn't that the system failed. It's that *I wasn't surprised*. I've come to *expect* it to fail under load. That's a terrible place to be.

We've become so focused on shipping the next feature that we've ignored the '-ilities'—reliability, scalability, maintainability. These are the characteristics that let us sleep through the night. These are the promises we make to our users that the system won't just *work*, but that it will work when they need it most.

That's why we're here today. Let's design a system we can all be confident in."

---

### Option 5: The Race Car and The Cargo Truck (Classic Analogy)

**(Techniques: Analogy, Dialogue, Raising Questions)**

"Let's say we built a vehicle. It's got an engine, four wheels, a steering wheel. It runs. We're proud of it.

Then we hand the keys to our users.

The first user calls us and says, **(Dialogue)** 'I tried to take this thing to the race track, and it was so slow! I got lapped by everyone!'

The second user calls and says, **(Dialogue)** 'I tried to haul two tons of lumber in the back, and the axles snapped! What junk!'

The third user says, **(Dialogue)** 'I just wanted to drive my kids to school, but there's only one seat and no seatbelts!'

Are they all using it wrong? Or did we just build a 'vehicle' without ever deciding what it was *for*?

A Formula 1 car, a cargo truck, and a family minivan are all 'vehicles,' but they are architected with completely different trade-offs. One is built for **performance**, one for **load capacity**, and one for **safety**.

Our product is that vehicle. And our users are all trying to do different things with it. Today, we stop tuning the engine and finally decide: what kind of vehicle are we building?"
